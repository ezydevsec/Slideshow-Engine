**Title:** I haven't launched my first app yet. Here's what it's already taught me.

**Cover image:** a plain dark card with the title in white. No app screens.

---

My first mobile app isn't live.

The launch is delayed. I'm building it around two jobs while clearing debt, and the honest answer to "when is it out?" is "not yet".

But a delayed launch is still a launch in progress, and I've learned more in the last few weeks than in the months before them. Most of it isn't about code.

This is for anyone building their first app, and for me a year from now.

### 1. The plumbing is the product

When I started, I thought the work was the screens. The workout view. The exercise list. The bits you can show people.

Those were the fun part, and they're mostly done.

What's left is the part nobody demos:

- Sign-in
- Subscriptions
- The flow from onboarding to paywall
- App Store submission

None of it looks like progress. All of it decides whether anyone can actually pay.

If you're early, budget for this part like it's half the project. It might be.

### 2. Every control has to earn its place

I removed two controls from my workout screen: an add-sets button and a ramp-up dropdown. Neither was broken.

They just didn't help. So I wrote one rule for the whole app:

**Every control has to shorten the next action or clarify a decision.**

If it does neither, it goes.

That rule turned adding a warm-up set into one tap. It moved exercise alternatives into the exercise list, where you're already looking. And it's why the app reads your last session and suggests your next target, instead of making you scroll back through old workouts to work it out yourself.

The best features I've built take a decision away. They don't add an option.

### 3. I chose no free trial

Most subscription apps offer one. I'm not.

A trial would get me more starts. But a trial count tells you who was curious. A paid plan tells you who was convinced.

When you're building with limited hours, you need numbers you can trust more than numbers that feel good.

The trade-off is that onboarding has to do the selling. Without a trial, onboarding is the trial. It has to show someone exactly what they're paying for before the paywall appears.

### 4. Write the rules before you write the content

Before I wrote a single piece of marketing, I wrote down what it isn't allowed to say:

- No promises about how you'll look
- No "your numbers go up every week"
- Every numeric example labelled "example"
- No invented users, reviews or results

Fitness marketing is full of before-and-after promises. They sell, right up until they turn into refunds and one-star reviews.

It's much easier to set those rules before the first post than to defend them after it.

### 5. Two accounts, not twenty

There's a playbook going round right now: dozens of TikTok accounts, hundreds of posts a day.

Maybe it works. But more accounts give you more posts, not more understanding. They don't tell you which message makes someone pay.

So I'm starting with two: one for the app, one for me. I'll add more when a format proves it repeats.

I've also had to face a less exciting question: who owns each account, which email it sits on, and how I'd recover it after a ban. If you're planning multiple accounts, answer that first.

### 6. Week one on X: seen, not followed

I spent my first real week on X replying to other app builders instead of just posting.

The numbers:

- 710 impressions
- 21 engagements
- 11 replies
- 5 profile visits
- Net followers: -1

Small numbers, but the lesson was clear.

**Replies get you seen. They don't get you followed.**

Someone reads a good reply, taps your name, lands on your profile and decides in a few seconds whether you're worth following. Mine didn't give them a reason. So this week I fixed the profile before chasing more reach.

### 7. Borrow other people's mistakes

Because I haven't submitted yet, I've been collecting other builders' rejections and payment problems and turning them into a checklist:

- Attach your subscriptions to the build you submit. One builder lost three weeks to that.
- Turn on billing grace period, so a failed card doesn't silently become a lost subscriber.
- Test a real purchase and a restore on a second device.
- Keep one way to reach your users that Apple doesn't control.

None of these are features. All of them cost money when you get them wrong.

### What's next

Finish the plumbing. Submit. Find out whether the onboarding can do the job a free trial would have done.

I'll keep posting the real numbers every Sunday, including the bad ones.

If you're building your first app too, follow along. I'd rather learn this in public than alone.
